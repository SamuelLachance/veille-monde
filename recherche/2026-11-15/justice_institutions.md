## 1. Which Security of Information Act / FISOIA or other espionage / foreign-interference prosecutions had developments in 2026?

### Takeaway
2026 was a bad year for Canada's flagship national-security prosecutions: William Majcher was acquitted on 13 May after the court found his arrest unlawful (Crown appeal filed in June), and the two FISOIA counts against CAF intelligence member Matthew Robar have disappeared from the official charge list ahead of his 9 December court martial. The Yuesheng Wang verdict was postponed without explanation, while two new cases surfaced: a Canadian NATO intern jailed in Belgium for alleged espionage (reportedly for China) and a federal scientist, Dennis Lu, charged with breach of trust after two decades of CSIS warnings.

### Inferences
- The Majcher acquittal and the Robar downgrade show the same pattern: dramatic national-security arrests announced jointly by police, followed by a much weaker case once evidence is tested. In both, the public account from the investigating agency (RCMP 2023; DND/CFNIS December 2025) is no longer consistent with the court record.
- The Majcher file documents an inter-agency contradiction: CSIS used him as a source (2011 onward), then branded him an espionage threat (2021), while the RCMP arrested him without knowing which was true.
- Robar: the official calendar (three NDA counts) contradicts the December 2025 DND release (eight counts incl. FISOIA) and November 2026 foreign media still reporting eight counts. No public explanation exists for the withdrawal.
- The Lu and Zhang cases both involve federal employers that kept or cleared a person despite prior red flags (CSIS briefings; a PSC fraud finding).

## 2. Which official publications, reviews and hearings in 2026 made notable disclosures — and what was delayed, redacted or contradictory?

### Takeaway
The official record of 2026 is dense but oddly muted: CSIS still names China, India, Russia, Iran and Pakistan; SITE saw no interference in the April by-elections; the new National Security Strategy names no state at all. The sharper disclosures came from review bodies (NSIRA found CSIS failed to report possible unlawful activity and poorly documents otherwise-illegal acts) and from committee hearings exposing a troubled foreign-influence registry (about 30 filings against 2,422 expected, two privacy breaches) and screening gaps behind the NATO intern case.
